Why this approach

Teams that need the same kind of intelligence work again and again usually have two ways to get it. Each has a real cost for jobs that come round on a regular basis.

General-purpose AI tools

They are strong for quick research, analysis and drafting, and for work you do once. For a job you repeat, the person using them tends to rebuild the process each time: supply the context, check the evidence and assemble the result. What a good run looks like stays in that person’s head.

Analyst teams and bespoke research

They bring depth and judgement, which matters for complex or sensitive questions. For a well-defined job that recurs, headcount or a fresh engagement each time can be more than the job needs.

The alternative worth testing

Choose the work you need, set the scope and the guardrails, and let a configured workflow do the repeatable part. Bring a person in where context and accountability matter. Start with one narrow job, and add others when the first has earned its place.

  • Repeatable jobs. You buy a job done, such as a watch, a comparison or a model. The AI is how it gets done and is not the product.
  • Your choice and a defined scope. You pick the jobs, and each one has stated sources, outputs and limits.
  • Clear expectations. Each job says what it produces. Price follows scope and is quoted before you commit.
  • Evidence and guardrails. A date and a source on every item, gaps shown as gaps, written rules, and no release without approval.
  • People where they change the outcome. At setup, on exceptions, and on decisions with consequences.
  • Start narrow, then expand. A first job does not lock you into a larger platform.

Where I come from

I am Mandhir Dua, an independent competitive strategy advisor with a background in travel marketplaces. I help teams out of analysis paralysis. The evidence usually exists, but it sits in separate places, and the patterns that matter are hidden between them.

My work is to pull those signals together, check that the sources are credible, and trace what a move leads to at the second and third order. I look at a decision through its trade-offs, its volatility and the options it keeps open: what we would lose, what would break it, which assumptions it rests on, and how to defend it. A fixed checklist gives a generic result, so I build workflows that connect signals across sources and test many possible futures, and I design them to take in a reviewer’s corrections.

What I would not automate is the judgement about people: how strong or fragile a relationship is, what it costs to damage, and what only comes from being in the room.

What I do not claim

I make no claim about accuracy, time saved or cost saved, and I have no client results to show. The examples run on invented figures. The next proof point is a real pilot on one job that measures the manual work before and after, the steps the workflow handles, the review it needs, what it misses or gets wrong, and its effect on the decision.

The brief for each job says plainly what is automated, what is entered by hand and what is reviewed by a person.
